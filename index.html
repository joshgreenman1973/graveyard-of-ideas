How to read this

Sources lists every link cited in an entry's text. Where a candidate's platform survives, the entry links to it or to coverage that quotes it. Otherwise it relies on contemporaneous press, later histories and Wikipedia articles that cite them.

Chapter sorts proposals four ways: eventually won (became law or policy in some form), ahead of their time (still debated, not enacted), probably bad and truly strange. The chapter assignments are judgment calls.

Fate is narrower. Adopted means implemented in some form, by anyone. Still in debate means proposed again or partly carried out. Dropped means rejected or abandoned, with no current push.

This is not a complete record. Many losing platforms, including David Dinkins's in 1993, are not covered. Corrections are welcome.

Patterns

What the list suggests

Adoption usually took decades.

Of the 8 adopted ideas, the median wait from the losing campaign to adoption was 20 years. Buckley's bike lanes and taxi plan took more than 40. Norman Thomas's call for unemployment insurance took six.

Someone else usually finished the job.

La Guardia's administration bought out the private subways Hillquit and Hylan wanted the city to own. Koch, who beat Abzug, signed the gay rights law she had promised.

The same ideas came back from different parties.

Buckley, a conservative, and Mailer, a man of the left, both ran on ending drug prohibition for addicts. Hylan, a Tammany Democrat, and Cuomo ran on city control of the subways a century apart.

Some ideas were tried and dropped.

Buckley wanted addicts quarantined until cured. Rockefeller started a commitment program a year later, and by his own account it failed.

Methodology, scope and sources

Scope

A losing candidate here is anyone who lost a general election or a major-party primary for mayor between 1886 and 2025. Henry George's 1886 race came before the five boroughs consolidated in 1898 and is included anyway. When a winner later adopted a loser's idea, the entry sits under the candidate who proposed it first, as far as the sources show.

Sourcing

Every factual claim on a card links to its source. Sources include campaign documents where they survive, such as Hillquit's 1917 acceptance message in the New York Call; contemporaneous coverage; histories from the Gotham Center, HistoryNet and the New York City Municipal Archives; and Wikipedia articles on each election, which were used mainly for vote totals. Proposals that could not be traced to a source were left out.

Chapters and fates

The four chapters (eventually won, ahead of their time, probably bad, truly strange) and the three fates (adopted, still in debate, dropped) are judgments about each idea, made by the author.
